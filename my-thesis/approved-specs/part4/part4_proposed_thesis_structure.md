## 1. Governing design rule

The revised dissertation must make one scientific chain visible from the table of contents onward:

**identify an economic problem → establish what theory and the literature imply → show what remains unresolved → state the bounded contribution → defend the data, assumptions, and empirical design → verify that models fit the observed data credibly → evaluate genuine forecasts → test alternative explanations and robustness → state what can and cannot be concluded → explain why the result matters for economics and policy**

This structure applies the Part 1 benchmark lessons and Prof. Verne's requirement that the thesis plan itself reflect and answer a clear and precise problématique.

The final written thesis must allow a reader to answer, without reconstructing the argument from scattered sections:

- What is the economic problem?
- Why do we not already know the answer?
- What exactly does the thesis add?
- Why do the data and method identify the relevant forecasting answer?
- What assumptions does the answer depend on?
- What evidence would falsify or materially weaken the argument?
- What alternative explanation could invalidate the interpretation?
- What do the results establish?
- How robust are they?
- What can and cannot be generalized?
- Why should an economist care?
- What follows for policy without claiming unsupported causality?

## 2. How Prof. Verne's earlier remarks constrain the architecture

The revised structure explicitly incorporates the following requirements.

### V-01. Two-part plan with two substantive subdivisions in each part

The thesis uses **two major Parts**, each containing **two chapters**. This gives the requested "deux parties et deux sous-parties" a visible formal expression.

### V-02. Economic theory must motivate the forecasting problem

The thesis keeps Fisher, Phillips/Phelps, Okun, Schumpeter, and relevant monetarist, Keynesian, neoclassical, and later developments only where they help explain why inflation, growth, unemployment, money, interest rates, and business-cycle turning points may become difficult to forecast.

Theoretical material is therefore neither removed nor allowed to become a detached survey.

### V-03. Forecasting must remain tied to current macroeconomic policy problems

The motivation must connect unstable inflation, interest rates, output, unemployment, monetary conditions, public debt/deficit pressures, geopolitical disturbances, and turning points to the difficulty of designing timely economic policy.

These contemporary issues motivate the question. They do not automatically become additional empirical target variables.

### V-04. Nonlinear models must be assessed against classical forecasting approaches

The revised design requires a clearly justified classical benchmark family before nonlinear models are evaluated.

Prof. Verne explicitly mentioned ARMA, ARCH/GARCH, and polynomial approaches as examples. Part 5 must assess which of these are scientifically appropriate for the actual forecast target and explain both inclusions and exclusions. The structure must not silently reduce "classical models" to one benchmark without justification.

### V-05. Recessions, expansions, peaks, and troughs matter

The empirical design must retain a visible role for business-cycle states and turning points because the policy value of forecasting is especially important near recessions and recoveries.

### V-06. Adequate fit comes before forecast claims

The empirical sequence must first establish whether a model fits the observed data credibly through fitted/predicted adequacy and diagnostics.

Only models that pass the approved adequacy/admissibility rules should be interpreted as serious forecasting candidates.

Adequate in-sample fit is treated as a **necessary screening condition, not sufficient evidence of forecast superiority**. Out-of-sample forecasting remains the decisive test of predictive value.

### V-07. The General Conclusion must answer the problématique

The conclusion must synthesize the theoretical and empirical results and answer the central research question directly.

### V-08. Titles must carry scientific meaning

Part and chapter titles must state the economic or empirical question being resolved. Generic titles such as "Theory," "Methodology," "Results," or "Robustness" are not acceptable as standalone chapter titles.

## 3. Central research problem

Macroeconomic policy depends on forecasts of inflation, activity, unemployment, monetary conditions, and interest rates. Yet the relationships among these aggregates can change during inflation episodes, recessions, recoveries, policy shifts, financial stress, geopolitical shocks, and other unstable periods.

Classical linear models provide discipline and transparency, but they impose comparatively stable dynamics. Nonlinear and state-dependent models are designed to represent changing propagation mechanisms, thresholds, asymmetries, and latent regimes. Their additional flexibility can also create overfitting, unstable parameters, implausible regimes, and poor forecasts.

The economic problem is therefore:

> **When macroeconomic relationships become unstable, does state-dependent nonlinear modelling provide forecast information that is sufficiently credible, robust, and economically meaningful to justify its additional complexity relative to appropriately specified classical forecasting benchmarks?**

## 4. Central research question

> **Under macroeconomic instability, when do nonlinear state-dependent forecasting models provide statistically credible and economically meaningful gains over classical forecasting benchmarks, and when does their additional complexity fail to improve prediction?**

Supporting questions:

1. Do the selected aggregates show evidence of persistence, breaks, instability, or nonlinear/state-dependent dynamics?
2. Can each model represent the observed data adequately before it is used for forecasting?
3. Do nonlinear models improve genuine out-of-sample forecasts relative to classical benchmarks?
4. Are any gains concentrated in recessions, expansions, peaks, troughs, or other unstable states?
5. Are the gains robust to transformations, lag structure, regime count, forecast horizon, cross-variable information, and other approved specification choices?
6. Can apparent nonlinear gains instead be explained by misspecified benchmarks, structural breaks, omitted cross-variable dynamics, or estimation failure?
7. What can the forecasting evidence establish for economics and policy, and what remains outside the design's causal scope?
8. Which findings are U.S.-sample-specific and which elements of the research design are transferable?

## 5. What the thesis is adding

The thesis must not claim invention of ARMA/AR, ARCH/GARCH, polynomial models, Markov switching, STAR, MSSTAR, or standard forecast-comparison procedures.

Its defensible contribution is a **benchmark-centred, instability-aware empirical evaluation framework** that:

- links theory and policy relevance to testable forecast questions;
- evaluates model adequacy before forecast performance;
- compares nonlinear/state-dependent models with appropriately justified classical benchmarks under common forecasting conditions;
- treats recessions, expansions, peaks, and troughs as economically meaningful evaluation states;
- keeps model failure visible;
- tests alternative explanations through threat-based robustness;
- separates predictive evidence from structural causal claims;
- distinguishes U.S.-specific model rankings from transferable research discipline.

Part 5 must verify the exact novelty claim against the literature before this wording becomes final.

## 6. What would falsify or materially weaken the main argument?

The revised thesis must allow adverse evidence.

The proposition that nonlinear complexity adds useful forecast information would be weakened if:

- nonlinear/state-dependent dynamics are not supported by the approved diagnostics;
- nonlinear models fail basic fitted/predicted adequacy or residual diagnostics;
- nonlinear models fit in-sample but do not improve out-of-sample forecasts;
- forecast gains are statistically weak or economically trivial;
- gains disappear under approved transformation, lag, regime-count, horizon, or sample checks;
- apparent regimes are empty, explosive, near-absorbing, numerically unstable, or uninterpretable;
- a better-specified classical benchmark removes the apparent nonlinear advantage;
- cross-variable information explains gains previously attributed to own-history nonlinearity;
- gains occur outside the recession/turning-point states that motivate the nonlinear interpretation;
- findings depend on a narrow U.S. episode and cannot support general model-ranking claims.

An ARMA or other classical benchmark remaining dominant is therefore a valid scientific result.

# Chapter 1
## From Fisher, Phillips and Okun to regime change: why inflation, activity and monetary conditions become hard to forecast

### Core question

> **Which economic mechanisms make the selected macroeconomic aggregates important for policy, and why can those relationships become unstable enough to create a forecasting problem?**

### Scientific function

This chapter supplies the economic foundation without becoming a history of macroeconomic thought.

### Proposed sections

**1.1 Forecasting as a policy problem under inflation, conflict, debt pressure, and cyclical instability**  
Explain why policy becomes harder when the expected paths of inflation, growth, unemployment, interest rates, and monetary conditions are uncertain.

**1.2 Fisherian relations between inflation, nominal rates, real rates, and expectations**  
Present the essential Fisher relation explicitly, define each term, state assumptions, and explain why unstable inflation expectations can alter the forecasting environment.

**1.3 Phillips-Phelps and Okun mechanisms linking inflation, unemployment, and output**  
Present the essential equations and explain why changing expectations, slack, and cyclical conditions can make historical relations unstable.

**1.4 Money, monetary transmission, and competing macroeconomic traditions**  
Retain monetarist, Keynesian, neoclassical/New Keynesian, and related developments only where they change the forecast interpretation. M2 remains conditional on Part 5 verification.

**1.5 Schumpeterian disruption, structural change, and turning points**  
Use Schumpeter only insofar as structural transformation provides an economic reason why peaks, troughs, recoveries, and transitions may not behave like normal periods.

**1.6 From economic instability to statistical state dependence**  
Define structural breaks, asymmetry, thresholds, smooth transitions, latent regimes, and the forms of nonlinearity relevant to the thesis.

**1.7 What the theoretical and empirical literature already knows, and what remains unresolved**  
Integrate the focused literature gap. Explicitly cover classical forecasting, nonlinear/state-dependent forecasting, multivariate nonlinear work, and mixed-frequency/nowcasting research at the level needed to delimit the thesis.

**1.8 Testable implications, competing explanations, and evidence that would contradict the argument**  
Translate theory into empirical expectations before any results are seen.

**1.9 Chapter 1 Concluding Synthesis: From macroeconomic mechanisms to a testable forecasting problem**  
Approximately one page. It must:
- restate the chapter-specific question;
- summarize the theoretical mechanisms retained;
- state the precise unresolved forecasting problem;
- identify the main competing explanations;
- explain why Chapter 2 must now define a credible empirical test;
- introduce no new literature, equation, evidence, or argument.

### Output

After Chapter 1, the reader must know **why an economist should care**, **why instability can matter for forecasts**, **why the literature leaves an empirical question open**, and **what evidence could challenge the nonlinear interpretation**.

# Chapter 2
## From classical benchmarks to state-dependent models: how to test forecast value without confusing fit, complexity and instability

### Core question

> **How can the thesis test whether nonlinear complexity adds forecast value while giving classical models a fair benchmark and separating model fit from genuine prediction?**

### Scientific function

This is the empirical contract of the thesis.

### Proposed sections

**2.1 From the Chapter 1 mechanisms to an empirical identification strategy**  
Explain what a predictive comparison can identify and what it cannot establish causally.

**2.2 Data, aggregate selection, exact samples, and frozen-vintage policy**  
For every retained variable: source, frequency, raw dates, usable dates, transformation, economic role, and reason for inclusion. Resolve the M2 issue explicitly.

**2.3 Stationarity, persistence, and structural-break evidence before model specification**  
Place decision-relevant tests in the main text, especially for unemployment and the policy rate.

**2.4 Testing for nonlinearity before nonlinear interpretation**  
Define the approved nonlinearity diagnostic stage and what rejection/non-rejection permits the thesis to claim.

**2.5 Classical forecasting benchmarks and what each benchmark tests**  
Give a scientifically justified benchmark hierarchy. Part 5 must examine the classical families highlighted by Prof. Verne, including AR/ARMA and, where appropriate to the forecast target, ARCH/GARCH and polynomial specifications. Any exclusion must be explained rather than ignored.

**2.6 State-dependent alternatives and their economic interpretation**  
Define the approved Markov-switching, STAR, hybrid, or other nonlinear models with only the equations required to understand their forecast meaning.

**2.7 Cross-variable information and the boundary of single-equation forecasting**  
Explain what own-history models miss and define the role of the focused Part 5 multivariate/cross-variable response.

**2.8 Fitted/predicted adequacy as a prerequisite for forecast interpretation**  
Define the in-sample adequacy and residual-diagnostic gate. A model that cannot credibly represent observed data is not promoted as serious forecast evidence. Also state explicitly that good fit alone does not prove forecast superiority.

**2.9 Forecast experiment: origins, windows, horizons, and re-estimation policy**  
Define a scientifically defensible and computationally efficient forecast experiment. Exact choices belong to Part 5.

**2.10 Forecast evaluation across normal periods, recessions, expansions, peaks, and troughs**  
Define the common loss measures, forecast-comparison logic, and state-conditioned evaluation.

**2.11 Model admissibility and failure rules**  
Predefine convergence, parameter plausibility, regime occupancy, persistence, explosive forecasts, and other failure criteria.

**2.12 Threat-based robustness plan**  
Map each proposed check to the alternative explanation it addresses.

**2.13 Reproducibility and numerical traceability**  
Keep frozen-data and output-traceability rules in the scientific narrative; move software internals to appendices/repository documentation.

**2.14 Chapter 2 Concluding Synthesis: From diagnostic evidence to a credible forecast experiment**  
Approximately one page. It must:
- restate why the design can answer the forecasting question;
- summarize the benchmark hierarchy, adequacy gate, nonlinear diagnostic logic, and forecast experiment;
- state the assumptions and limits that remain;
- identify exactly what Chapter 3 must test;
- introduce no new evidence or argument.

### Output

After Chapter 2, the reader must know **why these data and methods were chosen**, **why classical benchmarks are fair**, **what constitutes adequate fit**, **what counts as forecast superiority**, **what model failure means**, and **what the design cannot identify**.

# Chapter 4
## Do nonlinear forecast gains survive? Regime robustness, cross-variable dynamics and policy meaning

### Core question

> **Which baseline forecast gains survive the strongest alternative explanations, and what can the surviving evidence legitimately imply for economic policy?**

### Scientific function

This chapter attempts to invalidate the Chapter 3 conclusions before any policy meaning is accepted.

### Proposed sections

**4.1 From baseline result to falsification test: the threat-based robustness framework**  
Every robustness subsection follows: baseline claim → threat → check → result → implication.

**4.2 Transformation, persistence, and structural-break threats**  
Revisit conclusions sensitive to stationarity treatment, especially unemployment and the policy rate.

**4.3 Lag structure, seasonality, and classical-benchmark misspecification**  
Address the CPI lag issue and test whether an apparent nonlinear gain disappears when the classical benchmark is better specified.

**4.4 Two regimes or more? Testing the economic meaning of the latent-state structure**  
Give the required K=3 exercise a clear scientific purpose.

**4.5 Does model ranking change with the forecast horizon?**  
Address the President's concern that h=1 may be an unfavorable or incomplete test of nonlinear value.

**4.6 Does cross-variable information explain what own-history nonlinearity seemed to capture?**  
Report the focused multivariate/cross-variable analysis approved in Part 5.

**4.7 Model fragility as evidence, not a technical footnote**  
Distinguish genuine model inadequacy from numerical/estimation failure.

**4.8 What nowcasting and mixed-frequency methods could answer that this design does not**  
If outside scope, delimit the difference clearly. If Part 5 approves a limited exercise, report it here.

**4.9 What robust forecasting evidence means for inflation, unemployment, growth and monetary-policy decisions**  
Connect surviving forecast evidence to policy timing and monitoring without turning predictive results into causal optimal-policy claims.

**4.10 U.S.-specific findings versus transferable forecasting discipline**  
State what can and cannot be generalized.

**4.11 Chapter 4 Concluding Synthesis: Which findings survive and what they mean for economics and policy**  
Approximately one page. It must:
