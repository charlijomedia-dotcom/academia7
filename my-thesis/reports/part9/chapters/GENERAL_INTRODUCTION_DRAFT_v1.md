# General Introduction
## Forecasting Macroeconomic Aggregates Under Instability: Problem, Contribution and Research Strategy

The Book of Genesis recounts how Joseph interpreted Pharaoh's dreams as a forecast of seven years of abundance followed by seven years of famine in Egypt. Joseph advised Pharaoh to collect one-fifth of the harvest during the abundant years and store the grain for the famine. The narrative then reports that the seven years of plenty ended, the famine began, and the stored grain was distributed (*The Bible, Douay-Rheims, Complete*, 1998, Genesis 41:25–36, 41:53–57). In modern economic terms, this account describes a forecast-informed public buffer stock. Its central lesson is simple. Information about a future shortage can make preparation possible before the shortage begins. The account is used here as a narrative illustration, not as independent historical evidence that this was the first macroeconomic policy or that such a forecast was verified outside the biblical text.

In this narrative, the proposed grain reserve had value because the shortage was expected before it occurred. Grain stored after the harvest failed could not replace grain that had already been consumed. Yet a forecast alone was not enough. Pharaoh also needed the capacity to collect, store, protect, and release the reserves. The distinction still matters. Economic forecasts can inform the timing and scale of public action. They do not choose the policy, guarantee its execution, or remove uncertainty. This thesis studies the reliability of the information that precedes such decisions. It does not seek to identify the best macroeconomic policy.

Modern institutions face a less certain forecasting environment. In a study of gross domestic product (GDP) forecasts for 63 countries from 1992 to 2014, An et al. (2018) found that official and private forecasters often failed to anticipate the size of recessions until the recession year was nearly over. The problem was not confined to one institution. This evidence matters because a late revision leaves less time to prepare for a fall in output, jobs, or tax revenue. It does not, by itself, show that the forecast error caused the recession or that an earlier warning would have prevented it.

The inflation surge after the COVID-19 pandemic provides a more recent example. In his review of the Bank of England, Bernanke (2024, Table 7) reported that the root mean squared error (RMSE) of the Bank's one-year-ahead inflation forecasts rose from 0.64 percentage points in 2015–2019 to 4.60 percentage points in 2021 Q2–2023 Q3. External forecasts also deteriorated. Their one-year-ahead RMSE reached 4.67 percentage points in the later period. Bernanke linked much of the common forecast deterioration to large shocks, including the pandemic, energy prices, and supply constraints. These numbers show how difficult inflation forecasting can become during unusual episodes. They do not prove that one model or one central bank caused the surprise.

Fiscal forecasts also depart sharply from outcomes. The United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR, 2026, paras. 1.5 and 3.17) reported that its March 2023 forecast put public sector net borrowing for the 2024–2025 financial year at £85 billion. The recorded outcome was £152 billion, a published difference of £66 billion after rounding. The OBR attributed the gap to several factors. These included more persistent inflation, higher interest costs, and policy decisions made after the forecast. The difference therefore cannot be treated as a pure error in the forecasting model. It does show how much fiscal planning can change when the path of the economy differs from earlier expectations.

The human stakes are also measurable. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2023) reported negative real annual wage growth in 30 of 34 countries with available data in the first quarter of 2023. The average decline was 3.8%. Inflation reduced purchasing power, with particular pressure on low-income households. These wage losses were caused by economic conditions, not shown to be caused by forecast errors. Still, they show why advance information about inflation, employment, and activity is valuable to public institutions and households. A more reliable forecast may improve planning. It cannot eliminate shocks or guarantee better living standards.

Forecast errors create a further problem when policymakers must act on information that later changes. Orphanides (2001) showed that recommendations from monetary policy rules based on data available at the decision date can differ substantially from recommendations based on revised data. This distinction separates information that existed when a decision was made from information known only afterward. It also shows why good historical fit is not enough to judge a forecasting method. A method must be evaluated against information that could have been used at the forecast origin, within the limits of the data design.

The economic variables that governments monitor are linked, but their relations may not remain stable. Inflation affects real purchasing power and the information relevant to interest-rate decisions. Output and unemployment tend to move with the business cycle. Money and monetary policy are linked to broader nominal and real conditions. Fisher (1930), Phelps (1967), and Okun (1962) provide classic starting points for these connections. Yet changes in expectations, technology, financial conditions, policy rules, and rare shocks can alter observed dynamics. A relation that helps describe an expansion may be less useful during a recession or an abrupt policy shift. This possibility is the economic reason to study instability in forecasting.

Classical time-series models provide a disciplined starting point. Autoregressive (AR) models use past values of the target, while autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models also use past innovations. Their relative simplicity can support stable estimation and clear forecast comparisons. Nonlinear models allow a different possibility. Hamilton (1989) developed a Markov-switching approach in which economic dynamics can change across latent regimes. Smooth transition autoregressive (STAR) models allow the change between states to be gradual. Teräsvirta et al. (2005) showed that careful specification matters when such models are used to forecast macroeconomic series. These methods were well established before this thesis. Their existence is not its contribution.

Research since then has shown both the potential and the limits of nonlinear forecasts. Goulet Coulombe et al. (2022) found that nonlinear features explain part of the gains from machine learning in macroeconomic forecasting, especially in periods of high uncertainty. Clark et al. (2026) reported forecast gains from parsimonious nonlinear factors in a Bayesian vector autoregression (VAR) for U.S. macroeconomic data. Yet Naghi et al. (2024) found that several forecasting methods were competitive with random forests and that relative performance changed during the pandemic and later inflation episode. These studies use different models, information sets, losses, and samples. They support testing nonlinearity, but they do not establish that a specific nonlinear model will beat a sound linear benchmark in every series or period.

The evaluation method matters as much as model choice. Giacomini and White (2006) developed tests of conditional predictive ability, allowing relative forecast performance to be studied in different economic states. Hansen et al. (2011) showed why model comparison may yield a set of plausible models rather than a single winner. Rossi and Inoue (2012) studied the sensitivity of forecast tests to the estimation-window length. More recently, Harvey et al. (2025) developed an equal-average-accuracy test designed for loss differentials that may vary through time. Together, these studies show that a lower average forecast error is not enough. A reported gain must be examined for sampling uncertainty, time variation, model selection, and the conditions under which the forecast was produced.

An important question therefore remains for the present study: when does the added flexibility of state-dependent models improve forecasts enough to justify the extra estimation risk? A nonlinear model may fit past observations better yet fail to forecast new ones. It may identify a rare extreme value as a separate regime, assign nearly all observations to one state, or produce unstable parameter estimates. It may also appear better because a classical benchmark is poorly specified. Forecast gains must therefore survive tests of model adequacy, estimation stability, and alternative explanations. A linear model winning the comparison is a valid scientific result, not a failure of the research.

The central research question of this thesis is:

*Under macroeconomic instability, when do nonlinear state-dependent forecasting models provide statistically credible and economically meaningful gains over classical forecasting benchmarks, and when does their additional complexity fail to improve prediction?*

The study has three linked aims. First, it asks whether selected macroeconomic series show the persistence, breaks, or nonlinear patterns that could justify state-dependent models. Second, it tests whether those models improve out-of-sample forecast accuracy after their adequacy and numerical credibility are checked. Third, it asks whether any improvement depends on recessions, turning points, forecast horizons, data transformations, estimation windows, regime counts, or information from other variables. These aims concern forecast performance. They do not estimate the causal effect of a forecast on policy decisions or social welfare.

The United States provides the empirical case because it has long official monthly and quarterly series, several recession and inflation episodes, and direct links to Federal Reserve policy. The six forecast targets are consumer price inflation (CPIAUCSL), unemployment (UNRATE), industrial production growth (INDPRO), the federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS), M2 money growth (M2SL), and real GDP growth (GDPC1). The first five are monthly; real GDP remains quarterly. The National Bureau of Economic Research recession chronology is used to classify forecast errors after the fact, not to give a model information it lacked at the forecast origin. The data are drawn from a single frozen FRED/ALFRED historical vintage dated 5 October 2026. The approved research input also discloses two missing October 2025 observations handled by last observation carried forward. The five monthly targets extend to August 2026 and quarterly GDP to the second quarter of 2026.

The empirical design compares AR and ARMA benchmarks with parsimonious two-regime Markov-switching autoregressive (MSAR) and STAR models. It tests stationarity and nonlinearity before interpreting nonlinear estimates. It then examines in-sample fit, residual adequacy, regime credibility, and convergence before using the models for prediction. A fixed-length rolling design re-estimates parameters at each forecast origin. The baseline forecast horizon is one period; longer horizons test whether the conclusions depend on forecast distance. RMSE, mean absolute error, and relative out-of-sample squared-error measures describe accuracy. The Harvey–Leybourne–Zu procedure is the primary pairwise forecast test, while conditional predictive ability and Model Confidence Sets examine state effects and competing models. A focused monthly VAR, alternative transformations and windows, and an in-sample three-regime comparison address specified threats to the baseline results. Model failures are recorded instead of silently replaced with favorable estimates.

This design makes a narrower contribution than a claim to have solved macroeconomic forecasting. It combines justified classical benchmarks, alternative forms of state dependence, an instability-aware forecast test, and visible numerical failures in one reproducible comparison across six policy-relevant U.S. targets. It also tests whether apparent nonlinear gains survive changes in the assumptions most likely to alter them. Its main contribution is the evidence obtained under this common discipline, including evidence against added complexity. Its empirical rankings apply to the selected U.S. sample. The procedure for checking forecasting claims may be used elsewhere, but its model rankings cannot be transferred to other countries without new tests.

The final results do not establish a general advantage for nonlinear forecasts. At the baseline one-step horizon, neither MSAR nor STAR is significantly more accurate than the AR benchmark under the approved primary test for any of the six targets. This is a lack of evidence of superiority, not proof that the models are equally accurate. MSAR shows a nominal advantage over AR for CPI inflation at the 12-month robustness horizon, but that isolated result does not survive the multiple-testing sensitivity assessed in the final audit. Some nonlinear models improve in-sample fit, yet many rolling estimates are fragile. STAR forecasts can become extreme, and the three-regime unemployment comparison remains inconclusive. These findings show why estimation credibility must be considered alongside forecast error.

The dissertation has two parts. Part I develops the economic argument and its empirical test. Chapter 1 explains why inflation, activity, unemployment, money, and interest rates may follow unstable relations, and what earlier forecasting research already established. Chapter 2 sets out the data, models, adequacy rules, forecast design, statistical tests, and robustness checks. Part II evaluates the evidence. Chapter 3 reports the pre-estimation results, model credibility, baseline forecasts, and business-cycle comparisons. Chapter 4 tests the findings against alternative specifications, cross-variable information, estimation failures, and longer horizons. The General Conclusion answers the research question and states what the evidence can and cannot mean for economic policy.

A useful forecast need not be the most complex forecast. The practical question is whether its extra complexity produces a gain that remains credible when the economy changes. That is the question tested here.
