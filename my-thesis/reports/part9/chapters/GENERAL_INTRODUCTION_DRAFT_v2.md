# General Introduction

## Forecasting Macroeconomic Aggregates Under Instability: Problem, Contribution and Research Strategy

In the Book of Genesis, Joseph told Pharaoh that Egypt would face seven years of abundance followed by seven years of famine. He advised Pharaoh to appoint officials, collect one-fifth of the harvest during the abundant years, and store the grain. The account then describes the seven good years, the arrival of famine, and the release of stored grain (*The Bible, Douay-Rheims, Complete*, 1998, Genesis 41:25–36, 41:53–57). In modern economic language, this is an account of public buffer-stock policy based on a forecast. A shortage was expected. Grain was set aside while it was still available. The reserve could then be used when production failed.

The sequence matters more to this thesis than the type of policy Joseph proposed. In the narrative, the forecast came before the decision to store grain. Without advance warning, the same reserve could not have been prepared in the same way. The policy also required storage facilities, officials, enforcement, and a decision about when to release grain. A forecast was necessary to the story's success, but it was not sufficient on its own. The account does not provide independent historical proof of a fourteen-year forecast or establish that Pharaoh introduced the first macroeconomic policy. It offers an early narrative of a problem that remains central to economics: public decisions concern a future that cannot be observed when the decision is made.

Modern governments also make decisions before the full economic outcome is known. A government prepares a budget without knowing next year's tax receipts. A central bank sets an interest rate without knowing the final path of inflation and employment. Public agencies plan support measures before they know how severe a downturn will be. A forecast can help them prepare. Yet forecast errors can leave institutions planning for conditions that do not occur. This creates two distinct questions. The first asks which policy should be chosen when the future is uncertain. The second asks how that future can be forecast more accurately. This thesis addresses the second question. It does not estimate the best fiscal or monetary policy.

Forecast errors remain substantial even where institutions employ experienced economists and modern statistical tools. An et al. (2018) examined real gross domestic product (GDP) forecasts for 63 countries over 1992–2014. Their sample contained 153 country-years in which annual real output fell. The private forecasts made in April of the preceding year anticipated negative growth in only five of those cases. As the recession year progressed, the forecasts improved, but the size of the downturn was often missed until the year was nearly over. Official and private forecasters showed similar weaknesses. The study also found that strong expansions were missed. Its evidence points to a broader difficulty in forecasting large changes, not merely a tendency to miss bad news.

A recession forecast that improves only after output has fallen has limited value as an early warning. Employment decisions, public budgets, and investment plans often have long lead times. However, the evidence from An et al. (2018) must be read carefully. The authors use annual declines in GDP to identify recessions; this is not identical to the monthly business-cycle chronology used later in this thesis. Nor does a missed recession show that an alternative forecast would have prevented the downturn. The study establishes the scale and timing of forecasting failures. It does not identify the causal effect of those failures on economic welfare.

The inflation surge after the COVID-19 pandemic offers a recent example of similar limits. Bernanke (2024, Table 7) found that the Bank of England's one-year-ahead inflation forecast root mean squared error (RMSE) rose from 0.64 percentage points in 2015–2019 to 4.60 percentage points in 2021 Q2–2023 Q3. The RMSE for the corresponding private consensus forecasts reached 4.67 percentage points in the latter period. Table 1 of the review also shows large misses at other central banks. The European Central Bank's one-year-ahead inflation RMSE reached 4.99 percentage points during the same period. This was a common forecasting problem, not a failure confined to the United Kingdom.

Bernanke (2024) linked much of this deterioration to shocks that were difficult to predict, including the pandemic, energy prices, supply constraints, and changes in demand during reopening. The review also identified weaknesses in the Bank of England's forecasting infrastructure. Models and software had not always been maintained or tested as well as they should have been. The review therefore separates two sources of uncertainty. One comes from events that are hard to foresee. The other comes from the tools and procedures used to interpret available data. Better econometric methods may improve the second source. They cannot eliminate the first.

Fiscal forecasting faces related problems. The United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR, 2026, §3.17) reported that its March 2023 forecast put public sector net borrowing for the 2024–2025 financial year at £85 billion. The recorded outcome was £152 billion. The published shortfall in the forecast was £66 billion after rounding. The OBR linked the difference to persistent inflation, higher debt-interest and welfare spending, later spending decisions, and other fiscal forecasting differences. These components cannot be reduced to one GDP prediction error. The case nevertheless shows why uncertainty about future prices, output, and interest rates makes public financial planning difficult.

Forecast errors are important because the underlying economic outcomes affect people. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2023) reported that annual real wage growth was negative in 30 of 34 countries with available data in the first quarter of 2023. The average gap between nominal wage growth and inflation was 3.8 percentage points. Workers in low-income households faced particular pressure because food and energy made up a larger share of their spending. A surprise in inflation can also change the real value of wages, pensions, savings, and debts. These losses were caused by economic conditions. The OECD evidence does not show that inaccurate forecasts caused them. It does show why information about future inflation has practical value.

The same distinction applies to unemployment and growth. A downturn can reduce income and tax receipts while increasing demand for public support. A rise in unemployment may also occur after output has already weakened. If the timing and size of these changes are poorly forecast, the information used in budgets and policy discussions will be less reliable. This is an information problem, not a proof that any specific intervention would work. An accurate forecast gives policymakers a better description of the future under stated assumptions. It does not remove the need to choose between competing goals, respond to shocks, or assess the costs of action.

A further difficulty is that the data themselves change. Early estimates of GDP and other aggregates can be revised after more information arrives. Orphanides (2001) showed that a monetary policy rule can give different advice when it uses observations available at the decision date rather than revised observations known later. A model can therefore appear successful when evaluated with information that no policymaker could have used at the time. This matters for forecast research. The dates of the observations, the timing of publication, the estimation sample, and the rules for updating estimates all affect what a forecasting experiment means. Good ex post fit does not establish good real-time predictive performance.

These cases define the economic problem motivating this thesis. Forecasts are needed because policy and private decisions are made before outcomes are observed. Forecast errors are common, especially around large changes. Yet not every forecast error is avoidable, and a more accurate model does not guarantee a better policy. The scientific task is narrower. It is to test whether a specified class of forecasting models delivers more reliable predictions than simpler alternatives under observable conditions. Such a test can improve the evidence used to select forecasting tools. It cannot remove uncertainty from economic decisions.

Macroeconomic theory gives reasons to expect that some relationships may change. Fisher (1930) linked nominal interest rates, real returns, and expected changes in the purchasing power of money. Phillips (1958) studied the relation between unemployment and wage inflation. Phelps (1967) explained why inflation expectations matter for that relation. Okun (1962) examined the link between output and unemployment. These contributions connect the main aggregates of this thesis to questions that matter for policy. They do not establish that the same empirical coefficients will remain constant across every recession, inflation episode, or monetary regime.

Economic change can have several forms. A temporary shock may move an otherwise stable process away from its usual path. A lasting break may alter the process itself. The effects of a shock may depend on the initial level of inflation, unemployment, or interest rates. Financial stress may also change the speed with which economic variables return to their usual levels. These mechanisms are different. A model that treats every extreme observation as a permanent new regime may be misleading. A model that assumes one fixed relation throughout the sample may miss genuine changes. The relevant question is which description, if any, improves forecasts of future observations.

Long-term changes also complicate prediction. New technologies, institutions, regulations, and patterns of trade can alter the way economic activity is organized. Schumpeter (1934) placed economic change and innovation at the centre of his analysis of development. Modern forecasting studies have reached related questions through changes in trend, persistence, and volatility. Stock and Watson (2007), for example, examined why U.S. inflation had become harder to forecast and assessed the role of changing inflation dynamics. Lenza and Primiceri (2022) showed how the extreme observations of 2020 can affect estimation of a vector autoregression (VAR). These papers study different mechanisms, but both warn against assuming that the historical sample always represents a single stable environment.

Classical forecasting models provide the first answer to this problem. An autoregressive (AR) model uses past values of the target to predict its future values. An autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) model also uses past innovations. Such models do not need to identify an economic shock or estimate a full system of causal equations. They describe the serial dependence of an observed series. Their main virtues are parsimony, clear estimation rules, and a transparent forecast benchmark. A more complex model should demonstrate that its added parameters improve prediction beyond what these models already capture. Complexity is not a contribution unless it improves the answer to the forecasting question.

One approach to instability is to allow discrete changes in the dynamics of the data. Hamilton (1989) developed a Markov-switching framework in which an unobserved state governs changes in the distribution of output growth. The state follows a probabilistic transition process. An estimated regime can represent a period with different conditional means, persistence, or variance. Yet a statistical state is not automatically an observed recession or a specific monetary policy. Its economic meaning must be checked against the estimates, the share of observations assigned to each regime, and the stability of the transition probabilities. A model with two regimes is also not justified merely because the estimation algorithm returns two sets of coefficients.

A second approach allows change to be gradual. Smooth transition autoregressive (STAR) models permit the effect of past values to depend on a transition function. A transition can occur as an observed variable moves through a threshold region. Teräsvirta et al. (2005) examined the forecasting performance of linear models, STAR models, and neural networks across macroeconomic series. Their findings show why model specification and forecast evaluation are essential. A smooth mathematical function does not guarantee that a transition can be estimated precisely. If the threshold is poorly identified or the transition parameter reaches a boundary, forecasts may be sensitive to small changes in the data.

Recent research provides further reasons to test nonlinear models. Goulet Coulombe et al. (2022) separated the roles of nonlinearity, regularization, cross-validation, and the loss function in machine-learning forecasts. They found that nonlinear features were an important source of gains, with stronger benefits in periods of uncertainty and financial stress. Goulet Coulombe (2024) developed a random-forest approach to changing macroeconomic relations. Clark et al. (2026) proposed a Bayesian VAR with a small number of nonlinear factors. Their U.S. application found gains in density forecasts and showed how shared nonlinear features can limit the cost of estimation. This literature demonstrates that nonlinear flexibility can be useful. It does not establish the forecast performance of the specific Markov-switching or smooth-transition models examined here.

Other evidence shows why that distinction matters. Naghi et al. (2024) extended an earlier comparison of machine-learning methods for inflation. Several alternatives performed as well as or better than random forests, and results changed during the pandemic and the high-inflation period. A forecast model can therefore perform well in one sample and poorly in another. The data frequency, available predictors, forecast horizon, and scoring rule also matter. Some recent studies use large data sets and multivariate predictors. This thesis asks a narrower question about interpretable single-equation models built mainly from the target's own past. Their results cannot be inferred from the performance of machine-learning systems with a different information set.

A model may also be useful in one economic state but not another. Giacomini and White (2006) developed tests of conditional predictive ability that permit forecast comparisons to depend on information available when the forecast is made. Odendahl et al. (2023) proposed methods that allow forecast performance to change with an observed state variable. This work suggests that a model's average error may hide differences between expansions and recessions. Yet a regression that rejects a joint null does not necessarily establish that recession effects are significant. State dependence must be tested directly. Moreover, an ex post recession date is a useful label for evaluating realized forecast errors, not necessarily a real-time input available to the forecaster.

Even an average forecast comparison requires care. Diebold and Mariano (1995) provided a general framework for testing differences in predictive accuracy. Giacomini and White (2006) extended the evaluation to conditional objectives. Hansen et al. (2011) developed a Model Confidence Set to compare several competing models without declaring a winner on the basis of one small error difference. Rossi and Inoue (2012) showed that forecast-test conclusions can depend on the choice of estimation window. These studies make one point clear: the model with the lowest reported RMSE is not necessarily the model with a statistically supported advantage. A ranking and a statistical decision answer different questions.

Instability creates a further problem for the test itself. Harvey et al. (2025) examined forecast-loss differentials whose mean may vary over time. They showed why the usual equal-average-accuracy test can behave poorly in this setting and proposed a procedure based on local demeaning. This result is closely related to the present research question. If instability motivates the forecasting model, it must also be considered when evaluating forecasts. The test should not silently assume the very stability that the thesis questions. This is why the approved empirical design gives that procedure priority over a conventional comparison.

The literature therefore supplies many answers, but they concern different parts of the problem. Classical AR and ARMA models provide benchmarks. Markov switching and smooth transitions permit nonlinear dynamics. VARs, machine learning, and nowcasting methods expand the information or model space. Forecast tests assess whether observed error differences are credible. None of these results alone determines whether a two-regime MSAR or STAR model is more useful than AR for the six U.S. variables selected here, under one consistent set of estimation rules, fit checks, failure rules, horizons, and robustness tests. That bounded comparison is the empirical gap addressed by this study.

The gap is also methodological. A nonlinear model can appear promising because its in-sample likelihood is high while its estimated states are not credible. It may fit a single pandemic observation as a special regime, rather than identify a recurrent pattern. An optimizer can converge to a local solution. A forecast can become extreme even when the software reports a finite value. A classical benchmark may fail at an origin, which changes which forecast errors can fairly be compared. These issues affect the scientific meaning of a forecast ranking. They must be reported as part of the evidence, not hidden after the main results have been announced.

This thesis places model adequacy before forecast superiority. It asks whether the fitted structure is interpretable and numerically credible, whether the forecast was produced from an admissible estimate, and whether any gain survives formal evaluation. It also examines alternative explanations. A longer AR lag structure may absorb dependence that looks nonlinear. Differencing may remove apparent persistence. A VAR may show that information from other variables matters more than a nonlinear function of the target's own history. Different estimation windows may alter the ranking. A third regime may improve an information criterion without describing a meaningful recurrent state. Each possibility can weaken the case for added nonlinear complexity.

The central research question is:

*Under macroeconomic instability, when do nonlinear state-dependent forecasting models provide statistically credible and economically meaningful gains over classical forecasting benchmarks, and when does their additional complexity fail to improve prediction?*

Four questions make this problem testable. First, do the selected series show statistical evidence of nonlinearity or structural instability? Second, can the nonlinear models be estimated with adequate fit and credible state or transition parameters? Third, do their forecasts improve on AR and ARMA under the approved loss functions and formal tests? Fourth, do any gains remain when the study changes forecast horizons, transformations, estimation windows, regime counts, and the information used by the model? The study also evaluates realized errors around recessions, expansions, peaks, and troughs. These are comparisons of predictive performance, not estimates of causal economic mechanisms.

The hypothesis that nonlinear complexity always helps is deliberately open to rejection. If linear benchmarks forecast as well as nonlinear alternatives, the data do not establish the value of additional flexibility. If an apparent gain depends on one extreme date, an unstable optimizer, or a particular sample, that gain must be qualified. If a state-dependent model fits well but fails a forecast test, the fit cannot be used as proof of predictive value. Conversely, a clear and robust gain would support nonlinear forecasting for the specific target and conditions tested. These rules prevent the research question from being answered by choosing favorable results after the estimation.

The United States is selected because it provides a useful environment for this comparison. Its official records include long postwar series for prices, output, employment, money, and interest rates. They cover several recessions, inflation episodes, major policy changes, the global financial crisis, and the pandemic. The federal funds rate also connects the data to a clear monetary-policy setting. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis provides a reproducible route to the official series through FRED and ALFRED. This combination offers enough historical variation to examine changing dynamics while keeping data sources and variable definitions traceable. It does not make U.S. model rankings representative of other countries.

The six forecast targets cover five economic blocks. Consumer price inflation, measured from CPIAUCSL, represents prices. Real GDP growth (GDPC1) and industrial production growth (INDPRO) measure economic activity at different frequencies. The unemployment rate (UNRATE) measures labor-market conditions. The effective federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS) represents short-term monetary policy conditions, while M2 growth (M2SL) represents a broad monetary aggregate. These variables are policy-relevant and have long official histories. They also link the empirical study to the Fisher, Phillips-Phelps, Okun, and monetary mechanisms considered in Chapter 1. GDP remains quarterly, while the other five targets are monthly. The study does not create artificial monthly GDP observations.

The data are frozen at the historical FRED/ALFRED vintage of 5 October 2026. The monthly targets extend through August 2026 and quarterly GDP through the second quarter of 2026. Two missing raw-level observations for October 2025, in CPIAUCSL and UNRATE, receive the specifically approved last-observation-carried-forward treatment in the derived research input. The original source snapshot is unchanged, and the imputed values are not represented as observed data. This disclosure matters because a transformation can turn a carried-forward level into an artificial zero monthly change. Chapter 2 defines the treatment, and Chapter 4 assesses its effect on the reported forecasts.

The main comparison uses AR and ARMA as classical benchmarks and two-regime Markov-switching autoregressive (MSAR) and STAR models as nonlinear alternatives. The design first evaluates stationarity and nonlinearity. It then studies in-sample fit, residual behavior, parameter and regime credibility, convergence, and forecast admissibility. Forecasts are generated with fixed-length rolling estimation windows. The primary horizon is one step ahead. Longer monthly and quarterly horizons examine whether the answer changes with forecast distance. RMSE and mean absolute error describe predictive loss. The Harvey–Leybourne–Zu test supplies the primary pairwise statistical evaluation. Conditional predictive ability tests and the Model Confidence Set offer complementary evidence.

The robustness design has several limits and deliberate choices. A focused linear VAR examines whether cross-variable information changes the forecast comparison. Alternative transformations, CPI lag lengths, estimation-window sizes, and horizons test specific threats. A three-regime MSAR specification is estimated on the full sample as an in-sample check of the two-regime assumption. It is not used to claim superior out-of-sample forecasts. That scope follows the approved methodology amendment and addresses the jury's regime-count concern without adding thousands of rolling fits. Models that fail or become numerically fragile are documented. No replacement forecasts are silently substituted to make a model appear more accurate.

This design does not cover every forecasting approach used by governments. It does not estimate large structural models, directly assess mixed-frequency nowcasts, or recreate each historical real-time data vintage available at every forecast date. The NBER recession chronology is used to classify forecast performance after the outcome is known, not as an unobserved future input. The study also does not estimate whether a different forecast would have changed an interest-rate decision, a budget, or household welfare. These limits reduce the scope of the claims, but they make the empirical question precise and reproducible.

The thesis makes three contributions within that scope. The first is an economic comparison of classical and interpretable nonlinear models for six policy-relevant U.S. targets. The second is a forecast-evaluation design that separates model fit, parameter credibility, descriptive ranking, statistical evidence, and robustness. The third is a transparent account of unsuccessful or fragile nonlinear estimates and the effect of those failures on interpretation. The methods themselves are not new. The contribution lies in what this common test establishes, including evidence that does not favor the more complex models.

The results provide a limited case for nonlinear forecasting. At the baseline one-step horizon, neither MSAR nor STAR is significantly more accurate than AR under the approved primary test for any of the six targets. This is a failure to find evidence of superiority, not proof that the models have equal predictive accuracy. Some nonlinear models have better in-sample information criteria. Yet better fit is often paired with weak regime identification, sensitivity to estimation, or unstable forecasts. The distinction between fitting historical data and predicting new observations is therefore material, not merely formal.

There is one qualified exception. MSAR gives a nominal improvement over AR for CPI inflation at the 12-month robustness horizon. The improvement is small and does not survive the separate multiple-testing sensitivity reported in the final audit. The result cannot be presented as a systematic nonlinear advantage. The three-regime exercises also do not establish a credible third state, and the unemployment comparison remains inconclusive because the estimated three-regime likelihood is not a global maximum. Some STAR forecasts become extremely large. In those cases, degenerate test results do not provide usable evidence of equal accuracy. These findings limit the claim that greater flexibility is necessarily more useful.

The results also matter for the interpretation of the business cycle. A model that changes states is not automatically better at predicting recessions. The approved conditional forecast tests do not support a general nonlinear gain concentrated in recessions or turning points. Some descriptive rankings depend on the pandemic period and on the dates for which each model produces a valid forecast. The thesis therefore treats those rankings as sample-specific. A method should not be recommended for policy use merely because one table names it the lowest-error model. The adequacy of its estimates and the uncertainty of its ranking must also be considered.

The implications for economics and policy are modest but important. Governments need forecasts, but they also need to know how much confidence to place in each forecast. A complex model can be useful when its structure is supported and its predictive gain is stable. It can be misleading when a few extreme observations determine its regimes or when numerical failure drives its errors. The evidence in this thesis does not show that AR is always the best model or that nonlinear models should never be used. It shows that the added complexity of MSAR and STAR did not produce a generally credible forecast gain in this U.S. experiment. Forecast selection should therefore depend on tested performance rather than on model flexibility alone.

The dissertation follows a two-part, four-chapter structure. Part I moves from economic mechanisms to a defined forecasting experiment. Chapter 1 explains why the relations among inflation, unemployment, output, money, and interest rates may change. It reviews what economic theory and previous forecasting research already explain, and states the competing mechanisms that the empirical work must assess. Chapter 2 justifies the data, transformations, model families, estimation rules, adequacy checks, and statistical tests. It also defines the failure and robustness procedures before the evidence is interpreted.

Part II evaluates the models and challenges the initial findings. Chapter 3 reports the pre-estimation tests, in-sample adequacy, regime credibility, one-step forecast performance, and recession and turning-point results. Chapter 4 asks whether those conclusions survive different transformations, lags, horizons, estimation windows, regime counts, and multivariate information. It treats numerical fragility as part of the evidence and states the limits of generalization. The General Conclusion answers the research question and explains what the results can contribute to forecast-informed public decisions, without claiming to identify the best macroeconomic policy. The four chapters each end with a one-page concluding synthesis.

The question that began with Joseph's grain reserves thus leads to a modern empirical test. What matters is not whether a model can describe a changing economy in a complex way. It is whether that model can provide a more reliable forecast before the next observation is known, and whether the evidence for its advantage remains credible after the model has been challenged.
