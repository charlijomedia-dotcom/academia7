# General Conclusion

## What Nonlinear Forecasting Can and Cannot Contribute to Macroeconomic Policy Under Instability

### The research question and its answer

Macroeconomic decisions must be made before their consequences and, often, before the economic conditions on which they depend are fully known. Forecasts can inform those decisions, but their usefulness is limited when the relationships estimated from past observations change or when a model fails to anticipate an exceptional shock. This dissertation examined one proposed response to that difficulty: allowing the conditional mean of a macroeconomic time series to depend on latent regimes or smooth transitions rather than on a single fixed linear relation. The central question was whether such additional flexibility produces statistically credible and economically meaningful improvements over classical forecasting benchmarks under instability, and when it fails to do so.

The answer is conditional but predominantly unfavorable to a general claim of nonlinear predictive superiority. For the six U.S. targets and the historical forecasting procedures evaluated here, neither the two-state Markov-switching autoregression (MSAR) nor the smooth transition autoregression (STAR) establishes a statistically significant improvement over the autoregressive (AR) benchmark at the primary one-step horizon under the Harvey–Leybourne–Zu (HLZ) test. Some MSAR specifications reduce observed squared forecast errors appreciably, while others give a more interpretable account of historical variation. Those properties do not coincide systematically with reliable forecast gains. At longer horizons, there is one nominal positive result for CPI inflation twelve months ahead, but it does not survive the reported sensitivity to multiple comparisons. Additional nonlinear complexity therefore has not earned a general forecasting advantage in this experiment.

This answer is neither a demonstration that the six economic processes are linear nor proof that AR and the nonlinear alternatives have equal predictive accuracy. Failure to reject a forecast-accuracy null may reflect sampling uncertainty, heterogeneous effects, or limited power. Some STAR comparisons, moreover, are too numerically unstable to support ordinary statistical inference. The conclusion is instead a judgment about the evidence required to recommend an additional modelling mechanism: a favorable historical fit or a smaller sample error must be accompanied by credible estimation, comparable forecasts, informative formal inference, and sufficient robustness to plausible competing explanations. Under that combined standard, the broad case for the particular nonlinear specifications tested here remains unestablished.

### What economic theory explains—and what forecasting must still establish

The first part of the dissertation explained why this question matters without presuming its answer. The Fisher relation connects nominal rates to real returns and expected inflation, but expectations and financing conditions can change. The Phillips–Phelps framework connects inflation to labor-market conditions while making expectations and adjustment central to the relationship. Okun's relation links output and unemployment, although employment and production can adjust at different speeds across contractions and recoveries. Monetary transmission adds changing policy responses, credit conditions, money demand, velocity, and constraints on short-term interest rates. Schumpeterian innovation and institutional change offer longer-run reasons why historical relationships may cease to describe the environment in which a new forecast is made.

These mechanisms make instability economically plausible; they do not identify a unique statistical form for it. A lasting change in policy behavior differs from a temporary recession, a shift in volatility, a stochastic trend, a smooth threshold, or a single exceptional observation. The same large shock can be represented by a separate latent state in one model and by a disturbance or changing variance in another. A highly persistent but linear series may also be difficult to distinguish from one with changing coefficients over a limited sample. Consequently, the existence of economic change cannot, by itself, justify replacing a parsimonious forecasting equation with MSAR or STAR.

That distinction also explains why the findings do not rank the underlying economic theories. The forecasting equations use predominantly each target's own past. They do not estimate structural Phillips-curve or Okun coefficients, distinguish identified supply from demand shocks, or recover the causal effects of monetary policy. Statistical regimes are features of fitted distributions rather than observed economic institutions. A high-inflation state is not automatically a monetary-policy regime; an unusually negative output-growth state is not necessarily a recurring recession mechanism. The economic theories establish the relevance of the variables and the possible reasons for changing dynamics. Their quantitative predictive value must be assessed separately.

Existing research supplies compelling reasons to consider both sides of this comparison. Classical AR and autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models capture persistence and innovation dynamics with transparent parameterizations. Regime-switching and smooth-transition models allow forms of state dependence that those benchmarks restrict. More flexible nonlinear, multivariate and mixed-frequency methods show that some information or forms of instability may be useful in other forecasting settings. None of those findings determines whether the particular MSAR and STAR structures adopted here add useful point-forecast information for these six series under a common evaluation design. This dissertation's contribution addresses that bounded empirical question; it does not rest on claiming to have invented a forecasting model or discovered nonlinearity for the first time.

The methodological response was to put model adequacy before predictive claims. Chapter 2 defined the forecast targets and transformations, the initially selected model structures, rolling re-estimation, diagnostic tests, forecast admissibility, loss functions, and formal inference before interpreting the rankings. The experiment retained quarterly real GDP at its native frequency and used monthly series for CPI inflation, industrial production growth, unemployment, the change in the effective federal funds rate, and M2 growth. It employed the frozen historical FRED/ALFRED vintage of 5 October 2026, with monthly targets ending in August 2026 and GDP ending in 2026 Q2. The two missing October 2025 CPI and unemployment levels were explicitly carried forward for construction of the research input and examined in sensitivity analysis rather than represented as official observations.

A fixed-length rolling window of 240 monthly observations or 120 quarterly observations allowed parameter estimates to be updated as the forecast origin moved. The primary objective was prediction one native period ahead; additional horizons tested whether forecast distance changed the outcome. AR served as the primary benchmark and ARMA as a second classical competitor. The two-state MSAR represented changes in latent-state means, whereas STAR represented coefficients changing with an observed lagged transition variable. Separate checks examined conditional variance and a limited five-variable monthly vector autoregression (VAR). This structure was designed to compare specific sources of flexibility without treating all forms of instability as interchangeable.

### From nonlinear diagnostics to actual predictive performance

The empirical evidence first separates detecting a nonlinear feature from fitting an interpretable model. Initial-window tests reject selected linearity restrictions for unemployment, CPI inflation, and changes in the federal funds rate, although the results differ by test and target. No comparable nominal rejection occurs for real GDP growth, industrial production growth, or M2 growth under the stated procedures. These findings warrant examination of nonlinear alternatives for some series. They do not establish the suitability of a particular transition function or hidden-state model, much less its ability to predict subsequent observations. Conversely, a non-rejection does not demonstrate that the corresponding process is linear.

Full-sample estimates reinforce that distinction. MSAR and STAR each attain a lower reported Bayesian information criterion than AR for five of the six targets. Yet a smaller criterion is evidence about penalized historical fit, not about a credible, recurrent economic state or future accuracy. For real GDP, industrial production, and M2, the additional MSAR state captures very few effective observations, with problematic transition or covariance estimates. These cases are particularly sensitive to the exceptional disturbances around the pandemic. For unemployment, extremely persistent estimated transitions and uncertain state means weaken a literal labor-market interpretation. For the differenced federal funds rate, the two estimated regime means are indistinguishable. Several STAR fits likewise exhibit thresholds outside the central range of observed data, transition parameters at bounds, or unreliable parameter covariance estimates.

CPI inflation provides the clearest counterpoint. The full-sample two-state MSAR identifies lower- and higher-mean statistical inflation states, approximately 2.56% and 12.49% in annualized units. The higher-mean state occupies about 8.6% of the fitted sample, making its historical description substantially more interpretable than the nearly empty states in several other models. Nevertheless, the CPI fit retains residual serial dependence, and the distinction between those estimated means does not deliver a material one-month forecasting improvement. The corresponding one-step RMSEs are approximately 2.966 for MSAR and 2.967 for AR. The statistical representation of a historical high-inflation episode and the incremental prediction of next month's inflation are therefore different achievements.

The activity measures exhibit the reverse combination: a substantial descriptive forecast gain without adequate evidence of a stable nonlinear mechanism. Relative to AR on matched dates, the MSAR reduction in mean squared forecast error is approximately 29.2% for quarterly GDP growth and 23.6% for monthly industrial production growth at one step. These magnitudes deserve to be reported rather than dismissed. However, the primary squared-loss HLZ comparisons do not reject equal average accuracy in favor of MSAR, and the related model-confidence-set analysis retains classical competitors. The gains depend strongly on unusual pandemic-era observations, while the full-sample regimes supporting their economic interpretation are sparsely populated. A large realized error reduction concentrated in a few disturbances cannot, by itself, establish how the procedure will perform in a different crisis.

Unemployment demonstrates a different problem: the meaning of a forecast ranking depends on which forecasts were actually available. MSAR has the lowest one-step RMSE on its own retained dates. ARMA and MSAR also both show positive descriptive AR-relative performance when evaluated on their respective matched dates, but those matched calendars differ. An exceptional pandemic-period unemployment observation, together with unavailable benchmark forecasts at some origins, materially affects the resulting order. Neither model is established as significantly more accurate than AR under the primary test. The evidence does not support selecting MSAR for unemployment simply because it heads an own-valid-sample RMSE column, or declaring two competing methods equivalent because their errors are numerically close on a subset of dates.

For the monetary targets, the evidence is less favorable even descriptively to the estimated switching mean. AR has a lower one-step RMSE than MSAR for the change in the federal funds rate and for M2 growth, and the MSAR regime diagnostics do not support strong structural interpretations. These are findings about the particular transformations and model equations, not demonstrations that monetary dynamics or policy reactions are intrinsically linear.

The STAR results impose a further limit on the meaning of apparent forecast performance. Some rolling estimates produce extreme but finite predictions, resulting in extraordinarily large CPI and industrial-production forecast errors. Such observations were retained in the descriptive losses rather than hidden through truncation or replaced by another model's forecasts. When their concentration makes a loss test numerically degenerate, that test supplies no usable evidence of either equality or superiority. The valid one-step absolute-loss comparisons for unemployment and the federal funds rate, moreover, nominally favor AR over STAR. These outcomes show why obtaining a finite prediction and obtaining a credible forecast are not the same thing.

The business-cycle analysis answers another part of the original question. Under the AR benchmark, one-step forecast errors are larger in retrospectively dated recessions than in expansions for each of the six targets. Errors also tend to increase around historical peaks and troughs. These periods are therefore economically important sources of forecasting difficulty. They do not, however, establish the anticipated advantage of nonlinear models precisely when conditions are unstable. The state-conditioned loss regressions find no statistically supported MSAR or STAR improvement concentrated in recessions or turning-point neighborhoods. Some models have smaller descriptive losses near particular troughs, but those differences are uncertain and can change with the chosen neighborhood width. A joint regression rejection that includes the average-loss intercept cannot be reinterpreted as rejection of the separate state-effect restrictions.

The interpretation of this result is deliberately retrospective. The recession dates and turning points used to classify realized errors were not necessarily known at the corresponding forecast origins. Likewise, full-sample smoothed MSAR state probabilities draw on later observations. Forecasting an aggregate during a period subsequently identified as recession is not the same task as announcing a recession or its turning date in real time. The evidence here describes where the forecasting procedures encountered difficulty; it does not establish that a fitted state can function as an advance business-cycle warning.

### Which findings survive alternative explanations?

Chapter 4 was necessary because the absence of general one-step superiority might itself have depended on a weak AR benchmark, an unsuitable transformation, a restricted number of regimes, or the choice of estimation period. The robustness analysis was therefore directed at plausible threats rather than used to search retrospectively for a winning nonlinear specification.

Changing unemployment from levels to differences and the federal funds rate from differences to levels alters some descriptive rankings and forecast availability, but does not establish an MSAR gain against AR under the primary test. The CPI lag exercise similarly shows that own-history specification and loss functions matter. Among the four supplementary AR orders evaluated on their common calendar, AR(3) has the lowest descriptive one-step error. This does not prove that AR(3) is universally optimal, or that AR(12) is equivalent to the baseline AR(2). In particular, the study did not conduct a direct formal AR(2)-versus-AR(12) predictive-accuracy or equivalence test. The defensible finding is that the available evidence does not establish a forecasting benefit from replacing the baseline with the longer twelve-lag specification. Conditional-variance diagnostics also reveal dynamics that a changing conditional mean need not capture, without establishing a new point-forecast winner.

Adding a third latent state does not resolve the identification problem. For five targets the reported three-state MSAR has a lower full-sample BIC than its two-state counterpart, but none of the additional-state estimates meets the specified credibility requirements. The three-state CPI fit, for example, assigns only about 2.24% of observations to one state. The unemployment comparison is still more restricted: the obtained three-state likelihood is below the two-state likelihood despite the larger model's nesting, so its relative likelihood and regime-count verdict are inconclusive because of numerical optimization. These exercises are exclusively in-sample. They neither establish that two states are the true number nor provide an out-of-sample claim for three-state forecasting.

Forecast horizon is the main source of a qualified positive exception. At the twelve-month-ahead horizon for monthly CPI inflation, MSAR reduces matched squared forecast loss relative to AR by approximately 4.2%. The unadjusted HLZ test rejects equal average accuracy under both squared and absolute loss, favoring MSAR. This is an identifiable result for one target, one horizon, and the stated historical data. It cannot be treated as a revision of the one-month baseline or as a forecast of cumulative inflation over the next twelve months: the target remains the annualized monthly inflation rate at the later date. Moreover, the supplementary Holm correction across the 46 reported MSAR-versus-AR horizon-and-loss comparisons does not retain significance at the 5% family-wise level. The exception is consequently of interest for further validation, but it does not establish systematic nonlinear superiority.

The limited monthly VAR provides a separate test of whether additional information across economic aggregates matters more than nonlinear dependence in each target's own history. On some common evaluation calendars it improves descriptive error measures, notably for unemployment and M2 growth. Yet it does not significantly outperform AR under the primary test for any of the five monthly targets, and the federal funds rate absolute-loss comparison nominally favors AR. This result neither establishes that cross-variable information is useless nor shows that a latent regime was merely a proxy for omitted predictors. It means that the specified linear VAR does not supply a demonstrated, general alternative predictive gain. Quarterly GDP was not artificially converted to monthly observations for this check.

Estimation-window sensitivity makes the distinction between robust inference and unstable rankings especially visible. Across the declared shorter, baseline, and longer rolling windows, no nonlinear family acquires a statistically established gain over AR under the primary HLZ comparisons. Yet unemployment MSAR's matched one-step out-of-sample performance changes substantially: its AR-relative squared-error measure moves from approximately 0.24 with 180 months of estimation history, to 0.19 with 240 months, and to −1.10 with 300 months. The descriptive advantage is therefore not stable to an economically reasonable change in how much history informs estimation. The sensitivity of STAR forecast magnitudes to the same window choices reinforces the importance of examining the numerical procedure rather than treating the model name as a guarantee of adaptation to change.

This numerical dimension constitutes a substantive result of the dissertation. Pooled across the recorded estimation exercises, approximately two-thirds of two-state MSAR fits and four-fifths of STAR fits are classified as fragile under the specified diagnostic rules, although the incidence varies considerably by target. Fragility is distinct from outright failure: a retained prediction may be finite and scored even when its regime is sparsely occupied, its threshold poorly located, or its covariance estimate unreliable. MSAR estimates can also vary with local solutions and starting values; no global maximum is certified for the reported switching likelihoods. Classical methods are not free from failed fits, and a fair comparison must recognize those failures too. The practical lesson is not that every nonlinear model is unusable, but that model availability, identification, and forecast loss must be assessed together rather than selected from a favorable subset of successful predictions.

The documented October 2025 carry-forward construction presents a final, narrower data limitation. Shortening the evaluation before those affected observations does not plausibly reverse the main absence of general nonlinear superiority. It does not prove that the replacements have no influence on individual lag histories, parameters, or forecasts. Their effect remains conditional on the declared historical input. Similarly, the specification and window exercises test the alternatives actually implemented; they cannot rule out every other stochastic process or nonlinear architecture.

### Scientific contribution and implications for economic decisions

Taken together, the results make three connected contributions. First, they establish a target-specific empirical answer to a defined question about discrete and smooth nonlinear conditional-mean models. The answer recognizes statistical evidence of nonlinearity and some substantial descriptive advantages without mistaking either for general predictive superiority. The difference matters particularly for inflation, where a comparatively coherent historical regime produces almost no immediate forecast gain, and for real activity, where sizable historical error reductions coexist with sparse regimes and uncertain inference.

Second, the study demonstrates why the inferential object must remain explicit. An in-sample BIC, an own-valid-sample RMSE, a paired out-of-sample loss reduction, a formal HLZ rejection, and a recession-specific slope test do not answer the same question. Their distinctions become consequential when errors are concentrated in exceptional periods, available forecast dates differ, or several hypotheses are examined. An instability-aware primary test and a declared robustness plan help prevent a descriptive winner from becoming an unsupported scientific conclusion. They also prevent a statistical non-rejection from being called equivalence.

Third, the work integrates estimation credibility and forecast availability into the evaluation of forecasting methods. Sparse statistical regimes, local likelihood optima, boundary transitions, unstable nonlinear recursions, and unusable tests are not ancillary technical inconveniences when the purpose of a model is to produce a prediction at a particular date. Their incidence changes the interpretation of historical rankings and the confidence that can reasonably be attached to a model's forecast. This failure-aware approach is a contribution to how established forecasting families are assessed; it is not a claim to have introduced a new estimator or statistical theorem.

For macroeconomic policy, the findings are relevant to the quality and use of information, not to the optimal choice of an instrument. A central bank may need an inflation forecast at a specified lead time; a fiscal authority may need a forecast of growth and revenues; agencies may need early information on labor-market conditions. The present research indicates that it would be premature to grant a complex state-dependent model greater authority solely because it reproduces an inflation episode, a recession, or a pandemic observation more closely in-sample. A method should instead be considered in relation to the decision's horizon, the data actually available, the accuracy of credible benchmarks, and the reliability with which it supplies usable predictions.

Forecast accuracy is not itself a policy objective. The squared and absolute errors examined in this dissertation do not represent a government's loss from unexpected inflation, unemployment, or debt costs. A modest improvement in one forecast measure may have little effect on a decision, while a warning about an exceptionally adverse outcome may matter even when average point accuracy changes little. Further, the univariate models and unrestricted predictive VAR do not identify causal responses to monetary or fiscal interventions. Accordingly, the evidence cannot establish that adopting AR would improve policy welfare, that using MSAR would change the proper interest-rate decision, or that one statistical regime corresponds to an independently identified economic cause. Such claims would require a decision framework and causal evidence beyond this forecasting experiment.

The generalizability of the findings has the same necessary limits. The ranking is conditional on U.S. historical series, their transformations, the 5 October 2026 fixed vintage, the selected estimation windows and horizons, the included models, and the evaluation rules. It covers the U.S. episodes in that history, including the exceptionally influential pandemic shock. It does not measure the performance of these methods in other countries, nor does it reconstruct the publication delays and revisions that actual historical forecasters faced. Although each rolling estimate excludes its later target observation, a fixed-vintage pseudo-out-of-sample test is not a release-by-release real-time forecasting or nowcasting evaluation.

What can be taken to another economy or institutional setting is therefore not a numerical model ranking, but a sequence of research obligations: specify the economic target and information set; establish serious classical competitors; distinguish persistence, breaks, changing volatility and nonlinear conditional means; assess estimated states before interpreting them; record unusable and fragile forecasts; compare prediction losses on shared dates; test apparent gains with appropriate inference; and challenge them against alternatives that could plausibly account for the result. The thresholds and specifications used here are design choices that would themselves need scrutiny in a different setting, rather than universally valid economic constants.

### The next research question and the final perspective

The most consequential next step is to test whether a credible form of state dependence adds value under information available at actual historical decision dates. Such a study would reconstruct successive real-time data vintages and publication calendars, accommodate mixed-frequency and incomplete current-period observations where relevant, and retain transparent classical and multivariate benchmarks. It would evaluate the availability and stability of each forecast as well as its accuracy, while protecting any horizon-specific positive result against retrospective model selection and multiplicity. Independent validation of the twelve-month-ahead CPI finding would be particularly informative: its nominal success here gives a reason to test it again, not a reason to presume it will recur.

Other extensions could investigate different restrictions on regime transitions, more explicit treatment of exceptional shocks or changing volatility, or predictive distributions rather than point forecasts. Those approaches ask legitimate additional questions, but the present results cannot establish their performance in advance. The immediate priority is the information-set problem because the practical usefulness of a sophisticated forecasting rule depends on what was knowable when the prediction was required.

The account of Joseph's preparations for famine, with which the dissertation began, linked a forecast to a decision taken before the adverse outcome occurred. The distinction developed throughout this research is that a plausible account of future economic change must also survive a test of whether it improves prediction from information available before the outcome. More elaborate descriptions of past instability do not necessarily meet that test. The evidence reported here does not reject nonlinear economics or the potential usefulness of nonlinear forecasting. It establishes that, for the particular state-dependent models and U.S. point-forecast experiment studied, greater flexibility does not generally provide a sufficiently credible gain over classical autoregression. The lasting conclusion is thus both empirical and methodological: under instability, the value of a forecasting model lies not in the complexity of the changes it can describe, but in the reliability of the predictions it can substantiate.
